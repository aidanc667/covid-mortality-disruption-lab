# Findings

**COVID Mortality Disruption Lab**: real CDC WONDER data, 1999-2024. Full methodology and pre-registered hypotheses: [`research_protocol.md`](research_protocol.md). This document summarizes what the completed analysis found; it does not repeat the methodology, only interprets the results.

## The headline

Of the 6 major causes of death tested, **5 departed significantly from their pre-pandemic trend in 2020-21, and each stayed significantly off trend in the same direction for at least one later year.** By 2024 three causes were significantly off trend: cancer (+4.6%) above it, and Alzheimer's disease (-19.1%) and drug overdose (-18.0%) below it. Heart disease, stroke, and diabetes were back inside their expected range; diabetes in 2024 (+0.2%) was about level with its pre-pandemic trend. Alzheimer's is the one cause with no significant 2020-21 disruption; its decline only became significant in 2023-2024.

"Disrupted" and "significant" mean statistically distinguishable from a cause's normal pre-2020 year-to-year variation, not simply far from zero. That normal range is about ±1% for cancer but about ±7.5% for stroke, which is why cancer's +4.6% counts and stroke's +6.1% doesn't. Being within the normal range doesn't mean no effect, only that the gap can't be told apart from ordinary year-to-year swings.

| Cause | Result | p-value | Survives FDR | 2020-21 deviation | 2024 deviation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Diabetes | Persisted | 3.27 × 10⁻⁷ | Yes | +16.7% | +0.2% |
| Heart disease | Persisted | 3.86 × 10⁻⁵ | Yes | +7.8% | +3.0% |
| Cancer | Persisted | 1.94 × 10⁻⁴ | Yes | +1.7% | +4.6% |
| Drug overdose | Persisted | 3.36 × 10⁻⁴ | Yes | +26.6% | -18.0% |
| Stroke | Persisted | 1.69 × 10⁻³ | Yes | +8.8% | +6.1% |
| Alzheimer's disease | No significant disruption | 0.807 | No | +1.0% | -19.1% |

"Deviation" is the effect size: how far the observed rate is from the expected pre-pandemic trend, as a percent. This matters because significance and magnitude are different claims: drug overdose and diabetes show the largest 2020-21 disruptions (17-27%), while cancer, heart disease, and stroke are all real, FDR-significant, but modest by comparison (2-9%). All four of heart disease, stroke, diabetes, and drug overdose had their deviations reduced once their baselines were corrected (see the robustness section below). And the picture keeps shifting through 2024: drug overdose has fallen significantly *below* its expected trend (consistent with the real, documented 2023-2024 decline in overdose deaths), diabetes is back to about its pre-pandemic level, and heart disease and stroke have drifted back toward their expected trend.

"Persisted" means the cause's death rate moved significantly outside its expected pre-pandemic trend in 2020-21, and the 2022-2024 years that were still significantly off trend leaned the same direction. It does not require 2024 itself to be off trend: drug overdose, heart disease, and stroke are classified Persisted but were back inside their expected range by 2024.

## The two results that weren't supposed to happen this way

The project pre-registered a confidence level for each cause *before* looking at any 2020-2024 data (`research_protocol.md` §3). Two results contradict those stated priors, and that's exactly what makes them worth highlighting rather than downplaying: a result that confirms what you expected is much easier to have gotten by accident than one that surprises you.

**Cancer was expected to show nothing, and it didn't.** The pre-registered prior for cancer was explicitly a null result, with "low" confidence by design: the reasoning was that delayed cancer screening and treatment during the pandemic would take years longer than the 2024 data window to show up as excess mortality. Instead, cancer shows a significant, FDR-surviving disruption that has persisted through 2024. Either the deferred-care effect on cancer mortality moved faster than expected, or something else is contributing; this analysis can't distinguish between those, but the result itself is real and worth investigating further. Worth being upfront about: the wider literature on this specific question is unsettled in a way that cuts against a simple "screening delays are killing more cancer patients" story. Early 2020 models projected large future increases, but more recent modeling using actual pandemic-era England data found lung and breast cancer deaths came in *lower* than pre-pandemic trends would have predicted. This project's own small, real, persisting effect should be read against that uncertainty, not as confirmation of the largest early projections.

**Alzheimer's was expected to show a large effect, and it didn't.** The pre-registered prior was "high confidence" of a large disruption, on the theory that pandemic-era isolation and care-facility disruption would show up clearly in dementia mortality. It didn't: p = 0.81, nowhere close to significant. This doesn't mean isolation had no effect on people with Alzheimer's; it means that effect, if real, isn't visible in national mortality *rates* over this window using this method. A secondary check does turn up something real: pooling all five post-2020 years instead of just the acute window finds a significant later decline (p = 0.0019, averaging -8.7% vs. trend), consistent with mortality displacement: patients who would otherwise have died in 2023-2024 may already have died earlier in the pandemic. This is an additional finding, not a reason to revise the headline result above, which stays scoped to the pre-registered acute window (see `research_protocol.md`'s 2026-09-02 addendum).

## The negative control (validating the method itself)

Before trusting any of the above, the pipeline runs the identical method on a cause with no *direct* COVID mechanism: congenital malformations and chromosomal abnormalities, concentrated in infancy and driven by prenatal/genetic factors. It passed: no significant disruption (p = 0.68 on the gating metric, raw death counts). This doesn't prove every positive result above is real, but a failure here would have been strong evidence the method was just detecting noise or a database artifact, and it didn't fail.

Two methodological notes worth being upfront about. First: this wasn't the first choice. Accidental drowning was the original negative control, and it failed: it showed a real, statistically robust increase in deaths starting in 2020, confirmed on raw counts (not a rounding artifact). That's consistent with published CDC reporting on pandemic-era increases in drowning deaths (pool/beach closures, lifeguard shortages, more unsupervised time in home pools). Drowning was swapped out for congenital malformations because it was never actually COVID-independent, not because the method failed. Second: "no plausible mechanism at all" would overstate even the replacement control: prenatal and obstetric care (routine visits, anomaly screenings) was also disrupted during the pandemic, a real if smaller and more indirect pathway than the ones behind the 6 test causes. It's meaningfully more insulated than any of them, not hermetically sealed from the pandemic. Full account: `research_protocol.md`'s 2026-09-01 addenda.

## Robustness check: does this depend on modeling choices?

**A baseline correction, found through this exact process.** Heart disease and stroke originally used the same 1999-2019 baseline window as the other four causes, and under that window the quadratic sensitivity check below found their significance didn't hold up. Investigating why (§12 of `research_protocol.md`, 2026-09-01 addendum) found the straight-line baseline was measurably misdescribing both causes' actual pre-pandemic trajectory: both declined steeply through the 2000s, then flattened out, and a single straight line across the full period sits well below the real, flattened values by 2019 (17.7 points off for heart disease, 5.8 off for stroke, found using only 1999-2019 data, with no reference to what happened after 2020). A curved baseline fits this history far better, but was rejected as the fix: extrapolated forward it predicts *rising* rates through 2024 for both causes, the well-documented failure mode of polynomial extrapolation. A shorter, more recent linear window (2010-2019) does not have that problem, matches the flattened recent trend, and is now this project's baseline for these two causes. This does not make either result disappear: both remain significant, heart disease more so than before, but their reported deviation drops from an overstated 26-37% to a defensible 8-9%.

**A later correction for diabetes and drug overdose (post hoc).** Prompted by a reader's question about straight-line baselines, a check of how well each 1999-2019 line fit the last pre-pandemic years (2017-2019) found two more misfits: the line ran 5.2% below the actual diabetes rate, which had stopped falling around 2010, and 8.7% below the overdose rate, which had been accelerating. Both lines sat too low by 2020, overstating every later gap. Both were moved to the same 2010-2019 window. This was decided after all 2020-2024 results were known, so it's logged as post hoc in `research_protocol.md` (2026-10-19 addendum), with the 1999-2019 results kept for comparison. Both causes stay significantly disrupted in 2020-21 (diabetes +16.7% vs. +26.9% before; overdose +26.6% vs. +40.9%). What changes is 2024: diabetes goes from +15.0% (significant) to +0.2% (not), and overdose from -4.3% (not significant) to -18.0% (significant). Alzheimer's (+1.8% end-of-baseline gap) and cancer (-0.3%) keep 1999-2019.

With those corrections in place, three separate sensitivity checks re-fit the primary method one modeling choice at a time (`scripts/run_sensitivity_check.py`, surfaced on the app's Data Quality page):

1. **Baseline window**: all 6 test causes agree that a plausible alternate window doesn't change the classification (for heart disease, stroke, diabetes, and drug overdose, the alternate compared is the original 1999-2019 window, since 2010-2019 is now their primary).
2. **Significance threshold** (α=0.05 primary vs. a stricter α=0.01): 5 of 6 test causes agree. Stroke disagrees: at the stricter threshold, its individual post-acute years (2022-2024) no longer each clear a 99% interval, so the three-way classification shifts to "Resolved" even though the underlying acute-period p-value (0.0017) still clears α=0.01 on its own.
3. **Baseline trend shape** (linear vs. allowing the pre-pandemic trend to curve/quadratic), now compared on each cause's own corrected baseline: **5 of 6 causes are robust, 1 is not.** Heart disease is now fully robust to this check (p stays significant under both a straight line and a curve on its 2010-2019 window). Stroke is substantially improved but still not fully robust (p moves from 0.37 under the old full-range comparison to a much closer 0.096 under the corrected window, better, but still on the wrong side of 0.05). Diabetes, drug overdose, and cancer pass this check, as they did on their old baselines, and remain the most robust of the 5 disrupted causes.

Net effect: heart disease's finding is now on solid footing across every check run. Stroke's finding is real and much better-supported than before the correction, but it remains this project's single most uncertain "Persisted" classification, reported as such, not smoothed over.

Also worth stating plainly, since it doesn't show up in a p-value: measured lag-1 autocorrelation of each cause's own pre-pandemic residuals is moderate for Alzheimer's (0.65), stroke (0.50), and drug overdose (0.44), and low or negative for heart disease, cancer, and diabetes (-0.31 to 0.19). Heart disease, stroke, and diabetes dropped sharply after their baseline corrections (from 0.92, 0.93, and 0.82), since a straight line that misses a flattening leaves long runs of same-signed misses. The primary method's prediction-interval math assumes independent year-to-year residuals, which the remaining causes' baselines don't fully satisfy, meaning the classical p-values in the table above are more confident than a model that accounted for this would produce. This was a disclosed-but-unmodeled gap until a Newey-West (HAC) autocorrelation-robust version of the same test was added (`research_protocol.md`'s 2026-09-02 addendum): it moves p-values by up to about an order of magnitude in either direction, but **all 5 causes previously found significant remain significant under it**, a real correction, not a footnote to bury, and a reassuring rather than damaging one.

## Which counties were hit hardest

All five causes with a 2020-21 disruption have county-level data: each county's pooled 2015-2019 crude death rate compared with its pooled 2020-2024 rate, regressed against five County Health Rankings measures, with FDR correction within each cause. Counties are included when CDC reports a reliable rate in both periods (about 20+ deaths over five years): 3,066 for heart disease, 3,062 for cancer, 2,651 for stroke, 2,342 for diabetes, and 1,564 for drug overdose, out of about 3,140.

- **Higher smoking and uninsured rates go with larger disruption** for all five causes.
- **Higher household income goes with smaller disruption** for heart disease, diabetes, drug overdose, and cancer (not significant for stroke).
- **Higher obesity rates go with larger disruption** for heart disease, diabetes, and drug overdose (not significant for stroke or cancer).
- **Rurality is mixed.** More rural counties saw larger disruption for heart disease, drug overdose, and cancer, smaller for stroke, and no significant difference for diabetes.

**The rurality result changed.** An earlier version required a reliable rate in at least two individual years of each period, which kept only 926 counties for diabetes and 641 for drug overdose, mostly larger and less rural ones (excluded counties averaged 74-78% rural vs. 24-32% included). It found that more rural counties saw *smaller* disruption, significantly for diabetes and just short of significance for overdose. With pooled five-year rates, which keep most rural counties, that pattern disappears for diabetes and reverses for overdose. It was largely a product of which counties were left out. Both versions are shown side by side on the app's Geographic patterns page, and the switch is logged in `research_protocol.md` (2026-10-19 addendum).

**Exploratory: Alzheimer's disease.** It had no significant 2020-21 disruption, so it isn't part of the county analysis, but it was run the same way for comparison (2,426 counties). None of the five county measures survives FDR correction, and the median county's rate barely changed (-0.1 per 100,000). Its national decline came in 2023-2024, so a 2020-2024 average is a blunt measure of it.

Two caveats still apply. Counties left out are still mostly rural (83-94% vs. 45-63% included), though far fewer are left out. And county rates are crude, not age-adjusted, because CDC WONDER doesn't offer age adjustment at county level for its 2018-2024 database; rural counties tend to be older and aging faster, so part of any rural difference could be population aging rather than the pandemic.

These are associations, not causal claims: the analysis can't separate worse direct pandemic impact, worse deferred care, pre-existing vulnerability, or some combination. See `research_protocol.md`'s limitations (§12) for the full list.

## What this data cannot tell you

The project's causal-language policy (`research_protocol.md` §11) exists because mortality data alone cannot separate several very different mechanisms that would all produce the same statistical signature:

- Direct viral harm (someone died with or of COVID, miscoded to another cause)
- Deferred or interrupted medical care (missed a diagnosis, skipped a treatment, couldn't get a prescription refilled)
- Healthcare-system strain (hospitals overwhelmed, ambulances delayed)
- Economic and isolation stress (job loss, disrupted income, social isolation)

A "Persisted" classification for heart disease could be any of these, in any combination, and the mortality data can't distinguish them. That's why every result here is described as "associated with" or "consistent with," never "caused by."

## Data provenance

All national-level results use 15 real CDC WONDER exports (7 from the 1999-2019 database, 8 from the 2018-2024 database). County-level results use 10 pooled CDC WONDER exports (five causes, 2015-2019 and 2020-2024), 2 for the exploratory Alzheimer's county run, plus 4 year-by-year exports kept for the diabetes and overdose comparison and 2 heart disease 2018-2019 exports used to check that the two databases match county by county (they do, exactly).
