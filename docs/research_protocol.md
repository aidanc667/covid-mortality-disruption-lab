# Research Protocol

**COVID Mortality Disruption Lab — Multi-Cause COVID-19 Mortality Disruption Analysis**
**Version 2.0 — supersedes v1.0's diabetes-only design (see `docs/superpowers/specs/2026-08-31-covid-mortality-disruption-design.md` for the pivot rationale). Locked prior to any excess-mortality or heterogeneity analysis, per the project's confirmatory-analysis discipline.**
**Basis:** [`data_feasibility_audit.md`](data_feasibility_audit.md), [`../superpowers/specs/2026-08-31-covid-mortality-disruption-design.md`](superpowers/specs/2026-08-31-covid-mortality-disruption-design.md)

This protocol exists to reduce researcher degrees of freedom. Analytical choices below (inclusion criteria, methods, thresholds) are fixed before any results are inspected. Deviations discovered necessary during implementation must be logged in an addendum at the bottom of this file, not made silently.

---

## 1. Research question

**Primary:** Which causes of death experienced the greatest and most statistically significant disruption during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2024), how persistent were those disruptions through the most recent available data, and how did disruption severity vary across U.S. counties by socioeconomic status, healthcare access, and rurality?

COVID-19 is treated as a system-wide shock to the healthcare/public-health system, not as the disease under study. The question is deliberately scoped to *what* changed, *how much*, and *where* — not *why* — because mortality data alone cannot cleanly separate direct viral effects from deferred care, isolation, or economic-stress mechanisms.

## 2. Hypotheses

- H1: At least one of the 6 test causes (§3) shows a statistically significant 2020–2021 deviation from its expected pre-pandemic trend.
- H2: The pattern of significant deviations is not uniform across causes — some persist through 2024, some resolve, some reverse (three-way classification, §7).
- H3: County-level disruption magnitude, for causes with a significant deviation, is associated with socioeconomic status, healthcare access, and rurality. Explicitly associational, not causal (see §11).

## 3. Primary outcomes — pre-registered hypotheses per cause

Stated before any 2020–2024 data is pulled or analyzed, so results can be checked against stated priors rather than narrated after the fact.

| Cause | ICD-10 | Prior | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drug overdose | X40-X44, X60-X64, X85, Y10-Y14 (CDC standard definition) | Largest relative disruption of the set; may show reversal (declining) by 2023–2024 | Very high |
| Diseases of heart | I00-I09,I11,I13,I20-I51 | Real acute-phase (2020–2021) disruption; persistence uncertain | High |
| Diabetes mellitus | E10-E14 | Real acute-phase disruption; persistence uncertain | High |
| Alzheimer's disease | G30 | Real, possibly large relative disruption from isolation/care-disruption mechanism; persistence uncertain | High |
| Cerebrovascular disease | I60-I69 | Real but possibly harder to detect cleanly than heart disease (smaller N) | Moderate |
| Malignant neoplasms (cancer) | C00-C97 | **Expected to show little or no significant disruption in this data window** — deliberately included as the "lag" comparison case; true screening-delay mortality effect likely falls partly or fully after 2024 | Low (by design) |
| Congenital malformations, deformations and chromosomal abnormalities | Q00-Q99 | **Negative control.** No plausible COVID mechanism (deaths concentrated in infancy, driven by prenatal/genetic factors, not adult lockdown behavior or delayed elective screening). Expected to show no significant disruption. If it does, the method itself is suspect. | N/A — validation case |
| COVID-19 | U07.1 | Not a hypothesis test — reference/scaling series only (didn't exist pre-2020, trivially "disrupted") | N/A — reference series |

Each of the 6 test causes' age-adjusted rate (per 100,000, 2000 U.S. standard population) is the outcome, computed the same way CDC WONDER already provides it. Crude rates and raw death counts/population are retained alongside for suppression/reliability handling, not as primary analytic outcomes.

## 4. Temporal scope

- **Baseline (pre-trend):** 1999–2019, CDC WONDER "Underlying Cause of Death, 1999-2020" database (code D76).
- **Post-shock:** 2020–2024, CDC WONDER "Underlying Cause of Death, 2018-2024, Single Race" database (code D158). Confirmed via the live query form 2026-08-31: the Year finder lists exactly 2018–2024, no 2025 data exists yet.
- **Bridging:** the pre-trend is fit using 1999–2019 data only (`src/analysis/excess_mortality.fit_baseline_trend`). The entire 2020–2024 comparison period uses the D158 database exclusively — one consistent vintage. The 2018–2020 overlap years (present in both databases) are used by `src/cleaning/bridging.py` to estimate the size of the vintage discontinuity itself, so it is not mistaken for a COVID effect; `is_bridging_reliable()` must return `True` (median relative offset ≤10%) before results are trusted, per §9.
- **Geography:** national and state level for the core disruption/persistence analysis (adequate statistical power for every cause, including rarer ones like Alzheimer's, overdose, and drowning). County level only for the heterogeneity stage (§7's `src/analysis/heterogeneity.py`), using coarser pre/post period aggregates rather than full annual series, to manage suppression on lower-count causes.

## 5. Inclusion / exclusion criteria

**National/state-level disruption analysis:** no per-geography eligibility gate — national and state totals are large enough across all 8 series (including drowning and Alzheimer's) that suppression is not expected to be a material concern at this level of aggregation.

**County-level heterogeneity analysis:** a county is included only if it has at least 2 non-missing years of data in both the pre-period and post-period aggregation windows (`src/analysis/heterogeneity.compute_county_disruption`'s `min_years_each_period` parameter). This replaces the old single-disease `MIN_NONSUPPRESSED_YEARS`/`MIN_COUNTY_POPULATION` thresholds, which do not apply to this design's coarser county-level aggregation.

Counties failing this criterion are excluded from the heterogeneity regression only — they remain visible elsewhere (e.g. the Data Quality page) rather than being dropped from the dataset entirely.

## 6. Missing-data strategy

- CDC WONDER suppressed cells: never imputed to zero or dropped from the panel; retained as `*_suppressed`/`*_unreliable` boolean flags alongside a `NaN` rate (`src/ingestion/cdc_wonder.py`), excluded from trend-fitting and deviation calculations for that year/geography, never coerced to zero.
- Context variables (CHR&R, the only source actually used in the county-level regression — see §10's correction below): missingness is documented per variable, per county, per year in the Data Quality Report. No mortality value is ever imputed.

## 7. Primary statistical methods

1. **Known-date interrupted time series ("excess mortality")** — primary method. Fit an expected trend on 1999–2019 (`fit_baseline_trend`), project it forward through 2020–2024 with a prediction interval (`compute_deviations`), and flag a year as significantly disrupted if the observed value falls outside that interval. The breakpoint (2020) is fixed by the shock's known date, not searched for, which is more defensible against p-hacking than an algorithmic breakpoint search.
2. **Three-way persistence classification** (`classify_persistence`) — for causes with a significant 2020–2021 disruption: **Persisted** (still significant, same direction, through 2024), **Resolved** (shrank back within the prediction interval), or **Reversed** (flipped sign — e.g. overdose spiking then declining below trend). A binary persisted/resolved scheme would flatten exactly this kind of reversal finding.
3. **Cross-check via existing change-point methods** — three independent methods, all reused unmodified from the diabetes-pilot phase (`src/analysis/changepoints.py`): PELT, binary segmentation, and segmented regression (grid-searched breakpoint tested against a no-break null via a Chow test). All three run on each bridged full series to independently verify they land on a breakpoint near March 2020 without being told to. (2026-09-01 correction: the pipeline originally only ran PELT and binary segmentation despite this section always documenting all three — found and fixed during a self-audit; segmented regression is now wired in as the third vote.) **Disagreement between the primary method and this cross-check is not itself evidence against a "Persisted" result.** The two approaches answer different questions: the primary method tests one specific, pre-registered date (2020); the cross-check methods search the entire series for whichever single breakpoint best explains the data, which may fall elsewhere (an earlier slope change already underway, a later one, or simply no single dominant breakpoint if the disruption is gradual rather than sharp) without that meaning the 2020 date itself is wrong. On the real data, with all three methods now running, 2 of the 6 test causes' cross-check does not land within 2 years of 2020 (Diseases of heart, Cerebrovascular disease) despite the primary method finding a significant, large 2020–2021 deviation for each — this is presented in the app without being flagged as a contradiction, precisely because it is not one. Diabetes mellitus, which showed this same disagreement under PELT/binseg alone, is now confirmed by segmented regression (1 of 3 methods) — a reminder that a 2-of-3 cross-check disagreement can flip once a third, equally valid method is added, which is itself an argument for running all three rather than stopping at two.
4. **Negative control** — the identical pipeline (methods 1–3) run on congenital malformations, deformations and chromosomal abnormalities mortality (Q00-Q99), a cause with no *direct* COVID mechanism (see the 2026-09-01 addendum for why "no plausible mechanism at all" overstates this slightly). A significant "disruption" here indicates the method is detecting an artifact (vintage-bridging error, generic data-quality issue), not a real signal, and blocks trusting results on the other 7 series until resolved. This is a hard gate, not a footnote.

A breakpoint/persistence result is reported with a method-agreement summary between the primary (known-date ITS) and cross-check (PELT/binseg) methods, labeled explicitly as an analytical summary, not a formal probability.

## 7a. Multiple-testing strategy for the cause-of-death family

The 6 substantive test causes (heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, diabetes, Alzheimer's, overdose, cancer — excluding the COVID-19 reference series and the drowning negative control) are tested as one family; Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction (`src/analysis/excess_mortality.benjamini_hochberg`) applies across all 6 disruption tests. This is distinct from, and in addition to, the heterogeneity-stage FDR correction in §10.

## 8. Sensitivity analysis

Repeat the trend-fitting (§7, method 1) with an alternative baseline window (e.g., 2010–2019 instead of 1999–2019) to check whether the choice of pre-period length materially changes the expected-trend projection and, downstream, which causes are flagged as significantly disrupted.

## 9. Vintage-bridging reliability gate

Before any 2020–2024 result is reported, `src/cleaning/bridging.is_bridging_reliable()` must return `True` for the causes/geographies in question. A `False` result (median relative offset between the two database vintages exceeds 10% across the 2018–2020 overlap years) must be surfaced explicitly in the Data Quality page and the final report — not silently absorbed into the trend fit as if it were a real 2020 effect.

## 10. Heterogeneity analysis and its multiple-testing strategy

For each cause with a significant county-level disruption, `src/analysis/heterogeneity.compute_county_disruption` computes a per-county disruption magnitude (aggregated pre-COVID period mean minus aggregated COVID-era period mean), and `regress_disruption_on_context` regresses it against context variables (uninsured rate, smoking, obesity, median household income, rurality — all five from CHR&R's 2024 release; USDA and HRSA were investigated during the Phase 1 feasibility audit, per `DATA_SOURCES.md`, but were never wired into the final regression). Comparisons across context variables for a given cause use Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction as one family, separate from and in addition to §7a's cause-family correction. Raw and FDR-adjusted p-values are both retained; only FDR-adjusted results are described as "statistically significant" in generated report text. The county-level disruption magnitude itself uses **crude rate**, not age-adjusted rate, for both the pre-period (2015–2019) and post-period (2020–2024) — see the 2026-09-01 addendum for why (WONDER doesn't offer age-adjustment at county granularity for the D158 database) and its limitation.

**Context-variable vintage:** all five context variables are drawn from CHR&R's single most recent release (2024), not a pre-pandemic snapshot, and CHR&R's own documentation notes each underlying measure lags its release year by 1–4 years depending on the source survey (e.g. PLACES-modeled smoking/obesity estimates). Rurality is effectively time-invariant over this window, but income and the uninsured rate are not — using a single post-period vintage for variables that plausibly shifted *during* the disruption being explained (rather than a pre-pandemic baseline that would unambiguously precede it) is a limitation of this stage, logged in §12, not a temporal-ordering guarantee.

## 11. Causal language policy

No result may use "cause," "led to," or "resulted in." Approved language: "associated with," "temporally aligned with," "consistent with," "predictive of," "correlated with." This is enforced in `src/reporting/` text templates, not left to ad hoc phrasing.

## 12. Limitations (carried into final report, not just this document)

- **Observational/ecological design; no individual-level causal inference.** Specifically the county-level heterogeneity stage (§10) carries **ecological fallacy** risk by name: a relationship observed between county-*average* disruption and county-*average* context variables (e.g. uninsured rate) does not necessarily hold at the individual level — it's entirely possible for a county-level association to point one direction while the true individual-level relationship points another, or doesn't exist at all. Naming this explicitly rather than only implying it via "ecological design."
- **Selection bias in the county-level heterogeneity sample, confirmed on real data, cause-specific severity.** Counties excluded by the suppression filter (`min_years_each_period`) are disproportionately rural (77.7% rural excluded vs. 31.5% included for diabetes; 74.4% vs. 23.8% for overdose — see the 2026-09-01 addendum below for the full within-sample robustness check). The rurality finding is more trustworthy for diabetes (holds up, even strengthens, among the more-rural half of the included sample) than for drug overdose (driven almost entirely by the less-rural half; not significant among the more-rural half of the included sample) — this project's own most counterintuitive finding should not be reported with uniform confidence across both causes.
- Mortality-vintage discontinuity between the 1999–2020 and 2018–2024 CDC WONDER databases (§4, §9).
- ICD-10 cause-of-death coding practices may have shifted during 2020–2021 due to strain on death-certification systems, independent of true mortality changes — a real, specific artifact risk beyond the general vintage-discontinuity caveat.
- §3's pre-registered prior for cancer was an *expected null* result (measurement-lag reasoning: screening-delay mortality effects were expected to take longer than the 2024 data window to appear). The real result contradicts that prior — cancer shows a significant, FDR-surviving, still-persisting disruption (see `findings.md`). This is flagged here specifically so the prediction and the actual outcome are never conflated by a future reader: cancer was *not* found to be unaffected, contrary to what was originally expected going in.
- Small-county suppression and instability at the county-level heterogeneity stage.
- County-level heterogeneity uses crude rate, not age-adjusted rate (WONDER does not offer age-adjustment at county granularity for the D158 database — §10's addendum), so measured disruption magnitude may partly reflect each county's own population-aging trajectory rather than a COVID-era shift, and this is plausibly correlated with rurality itself.
- PLACES model-based behavioral estimates (CHR&R smoking/obesity/inactivity).
- **Context-variable vintage is post-period, not pre-period.** All five §10 context variables come from CHR&R's 2024 release, measured during or after the 2020–2024 disruption window they're being regressed against, not a pre-pandemic baseline. Rurality is effectively static, but income and the uninsured rate plausibly moved *during* the pandemic — this stage cannot rule out that some of the measured association reflects the disruption's own economic aftermath feeding back into the context variable, rather than a pre-existing county characteristic predicting the disruption.
- Multiple testing across both the 6-cause family (§7a) and, separately, the context-variable family per cause (§10).
- Spatial non-independence of counties.
- **Temporal autocorrelation of baseline residuals: `compute_acute_pvalue`'s classical p-value assumes independence and is optimistic; a corrected version is now also reported.** Measured lag-1 autocorrelation of each cause's own baseline residuals (`src/analysis/excess_mortality.compute_residual_autocorrelation`): Diabetes mellitus 0.82, Drug overdose 0.75, Alzheimer's disease 0.65, Malignant neoplasms 0.19, Cerebrovascular disease 0.50, Diseases of heart 0.12 (the last two measured on their corrected 2010–2019 baseline — see the baseline-correction addendum below; on the original full-range baseline they were 0.93 and 0.92 respectively). This was originally left as a disclosed-but-unmodeled caveat; the 2026-09-02 addendum below builds and reports a Newey-West (HAC) autocorrelation-robust version of the same test alongside the classical one (`hac_p_value`, shown in the app as "Autocorrelation-robust p-value"). The corrected p-values are meaningfully larger for the high-autocorrelation causes (diabetes, drug overdose, cerebrovascular disease rise by roughly 1-2 orders of magnitude) but **all 5 previously-significant causes remain significant** under the correction — this is not a false-positive problem, but the classical p-values should be read as optimistic, and the HAC column as the more honest estimate.
- **Effect size, not just significance:** the 6 test causes vary enormously in *how large* their disruption is, which raw p-values don't communicate. Mean 2020–2021 deviation from expected trend, as a percent: Drug overdose +40.9%, Diabetes mellitus +26.9%, Cerebrovascular disease +8.8%, Diseases of heart +7.8%, Malignant neoplasms +1.7%, Alzheimer's disease +1.0% (not significant). Drug overdose and diabetes are the clearly large disruptions; cancer, heart disease, and cerebrovascular disease are all real and FDR-significant but modest by comparison — "significant" and "large" are not the same claim, and this project's headline results should not conflate them. (Heart disease and cerebrovascular disease's deviations dropped substantially from an earlier, uncorrected +26.1%/+37.0% once their baseline was corrected — see below.) By 2024 the picture keeps shifting: drug overdose has swung to -4.3% (below its expected trend, consistent with 2023-2024's real decline in overdose deaths), while heart disease and cerebrovascular disease have both drifted slightly toward their expected trend, not away from it.
- All findings remain associational; the "why" behind any confirmed disruption (direct viral effect vs. deferred care vs. isolation vs. economic stress) cannot be cleanly separated by mortality data alone.
- **Alzheimer's null result may partly reflect a definitional gap in this project's own method, not an absence of any real effect.** This project counts a death only when Alzheimer's disease is coded as the sole *underlying* cause (standard CDC WONDER "Underlying Cause of Death" query, per §3). Published national research that instead counts Alzheimer's/related dementias as an underlying *or contributing* cause found a large excess: an estimated 94,688 excess deaths with ADRD involved in the pandemic's first year alone (Xu et al., *JAMA Neurology*, 2023), consistent with the project's original high-confidence prior. Many dementia patients who died of COVID-19 likely had COVID-19, not Alzheimer's, recorded as the underlying cause — invisible to a query that only looks at underlying cause. That same research also found the excess declined sharply from pandemic year 1 to year 2, consistent with a real but short-lived effect this project's 2020-2021 acute-year window could plausibly have partially missed. This project's "No significant disruption" describes what its specific method found, not that isolation/care-facility disruption had no real effect on people with Alzheimer's.

## Addenda (deviations discovered during implementation)

**2026-08-29 — Trajectory classification threshold (brief §15).** A county's post-breakpoint trajectory is classified as:
- **Improving**: segmented regression detects a significant break (§7) AND `slope_diff <= -0.3` (age-adjusted deaths per 100k per year)
- **Worsening**: significant break AND `slope_diff >= +0.3`
- **Stable**: either no significant break is detected, or a significant break with `|slope_diff| < 0.3`

The ±0.3/100k/year threshold is a placeholder magnitude chosen to separate visually/practically meaningful slope changes from noise-level ones, pending calibration against the real mortality series once available (candidate calibration: set the threshold from the empirical distribution of `slope_diff` across all eligible counties, e.g. its interquartile range, rather than a fixed constant). This must be revisited before any results derived from it are reported as final — flagged here so it isn't mistaken for an a priori confirmatory choice.

**2026-08-29 — Change-point detection on the diff series (implementation note, not a protocol change).** `ruptures`' standard cost models detect shifts in mean, not in trend/slope. Since a diabetes-mortality breakpoint is a slope change, PELT and binary segmentation are run on the first-differenced rate series (`src/analysis/changepoints.py`), which turns a slope change into a mean shift; breakpoint years are then mapped back to the original series. This does not change the methods specified in §7, only their correct implementation.

**2026-08-31 — Pivot from single-disease breakpoint detection to multi-cause COVID disruption analysis.** This protocol was rewritten wholesale (v1.0 → v2.0) per `docs/superpowers/specs/2026-08-31-covid-mortality-disruption-design.md`. The diabetes-only research question, its algorithmic-breakpoint-search primary method, and its trajectory-classification thresholds (both addenda above) are retired as the primary design, though the underlying code they describe (`src/analysis/changepoints.py`) is retained and reused as an independent cross-check under the new design (§7, method 3). Diabetes itself is retained as one of the 6 test causes under the new design (§3), not dropped.

**2026-09-01 — Real data acquisition: multi-cause bundling abandoned, state-level deferred.** Two real acquisition attempts confirmed that requesting multiple causes in one WONDER query does not work as designed (see `docs/manual_data_acquisition.md`'s corrected instructions for the full account): with no cause-grouping, WONDER silently blends all selected causes into one combined number; with "And By: Cause of death" grouping, it explodes each category into every individual 4-digit ICD-10 sub-code instead of one row per selected category. The only reliable method is one cause per query. Given that constraint multiplies the export count, **state-level acquisition is deferred** (not abandoned) — none of H1/H2/H3 require it: H1/H2 are tested at the national level, H3 at the county level, so state was always an intermediate-resolution addition, not load-bearing for the core claims. §4/§5's geography language should be read as "national (primary, in progress) and county (heterogeneity stage, primary) — state deferred" until revisited.

**2026-09-01 — D158 export year range widened to include the vintage-bridging overlap.** The 15-export national data-acquisition plan originally specified D158 (post-shock) exports for 2020–2024 only, which has zero overlap with D76's 1999–2019 baseline — leaving `src/cleaning/bridging.is_bridging_reliable` (§9's hard gate) with no shared years to calibrate against. Corrected: all D158 exports now pull **2018–2024** (the database's full available range) instead of 2020–2024, at no extra query cost, so 2018–2019 exists in both databases for bridging calibration. The excess-mortality analysis itself still only treats 2020–2024 as the "post" period; the extra 2018–2019 rows are used solely for the bridging check.

**2026-09-01 — Negative control swapped from accidental drowning to congenital malformations.** Running the real pipeline (§3, §7 method 4) on the first 15 real national exports found accidental drowning (W65-W74) fails the negative-control hard gate: it shows a statistically significant, persistent increase from 2020 onward (`classify_persistence` → "Persisted", p=1.2e-05 on raw death counts, p=7.2e-05 on age-adjusted rate — confirmed genuine by re-testing on raw counts, which rules out a rounding-precision artifact from WONDER's 1-decimal age-adjusted-rate reporting for this low-magnitude cause). This is consistent with published literature (CDC MMWR documented real COVID-era increases in drowning deaths from pool/beach closures, lifeguard shortages, and increased unsupervised time in home pools) — drowning was never actually COVID-independent, so it was a flawed choice of negative control, not evidence the method itself is broken. §3 and §7 method 4 are corrected to use congenital malformations, deformations and chromosomal abnormalities (Q00-Q99) instead — deaths concentrated in infancy, driven by prenatal/genetic factors, with no plausible mechanistic link to adult lockdown behavior, delayed elective screening, or COVID-comorbidity/miscoding. The drowning finding itself is retained as a documented, real, citable side-observation (not discarded), but is no longer part of the pre-registered hypothesis family or the pipeline's active negative-control gate.

**2026-09-01 — County-level heterogeneity uses crude rate, not age-adjusted rate, for both periods.** The first real county-level pull (diabetes, D158 2020–2024, grouped by County) confirmed CDC WONDER does not offer Age-Adjusted Rate at all when a query is grouped by County against the D158 (2018–2024, Single Race) database — not per-cell suppression, but a whole-file capability gap (no "Standard Population" line in the query parameters, no Age Adjusted Rate columns at all), most likely because single-race population estimates lack the age-stratified denominators needed to standardize at county granularity. The D76 (1999–2019) county-level pull for the same cause *does* have it. Since the heterogeneity stage's "disruption" is `mean(post) - mean(pre)`, using age-adjusted rate for one period and crude rate for the other would not be comparable. §7's `src/analysis/heterogeneity.compute_county_disruption` and `regress_disruption_on_context` are corrected to use `crude_rate` consistently for both periods at the county-level heterogeneity stage only — the 6 national test causes and the negative control are unaffected (both national-level pulls have age-adjusted rate available). This is a real limitation, not just an implementation detail: crude rate does not control for a county's population age structure, so part of any measured "disruption" could reflect each county's own population-aging trajectory over 2015–2024 rather than a COVID-era mortality shift — and that confound is plausibly correlated with rurality itself (rural counties disproportionately age due to youth outmigration), one of the very context variables being tested in §10. This is now logged in §12's limitations, not silently absorbed into the regression as if it were a clean association.

**2026-09-01 — Sensitivity analysis (§8) run against real data, three axes: 2 of 6 causes are NOT robust to trend shape.** `scripts/run_sensitivity_check.py` re-fits the primary method three separate ways, varying one modeling choice at a time against the primary result:

1. **Baseline window** (1999–2019 primary vs. 2010–2019, shorter/more recent): all 6 test causes classify identically. p-values tighten under the shorter window; no sign or significance flips.
2. **Significance threshold** (α=0.05 primary vs. α=0.01, stricter): all 6 test causes classify identically — every p-value that clears 0.05 also clears 0.01 by a wide margin (largest surviving p-value is 1.4×10⁻³).
3. **Baseline trend shape** (linear primary vs. quadratic, allowing the pre-pandemic trend to curve rather than forcing a straight line): **4 of 6 causes are robust, 2 are not.** Diabetes mellitus, Drug overdose, and Malignant neoplasms remain significant under a quadratic baseline (p stays under 0.001 for all three); Alzheimer's disease remains non-significant, consistent with the primary result. But **Diseases of heart (p: 6.3×10⁻⁴ → 0.061) and Cerebrovascular disease (p: 1.4×10⁻³ → 0.37) both lose significance** when the baseline trend is allowed to curve instead of being forced linear. This means part of what the primary (linear) method reads as a 2020 disruption for these two causes could instead be the natural curvature of their pre-existing trend, poorly extrapolated by a straight line — the linear model may be too rigid for these two causes specifically. This is a real, material finding, not a technicality: **of the 5 "Persisted" results, heart disease and cerebrovascular disease are the least robust; diabetes, drug overdose, and malignant neoplasms are the most robust**, holding up across all three sensitivity axes tested. Reported transparently in `findings.md` and the app's Data Quality page, not smoothed over.

Negative control note: its age-adjusted-rate row does flip across baseline windows (axis 1), but this reproduces the already-documented rounding artifact (see the negative-control-swap addendum above), not a new instability — re-run on its actual gate metric (raw death counts), it is stable ("No significant disruption" in both windows, p=0.68 → p=0.10).

Output: `outputs/models/sensitivity_check.parquet`.

**2026-09-01 — Negative control's gate decision uses raw death counts, not age-adjusted rate.** The first congenital-malformations pull (see above) initially also failed the hard gate on age-adjusted rate (p=0.013, "Persisted"). Investigation found this is a rounding-precision artifact, not a real effect: CDC WONDER reports this cause's age-adjusted rate to only 1 decimal, and at its low magnitude (~3.0–3.8/100k) that quantization makes the 1999–2019 OLS baseline fit artificially tight (`residual_std=0.076`), so even a ~0.1–0.4/100k gap between the flat 2020–2024 rate and a mildly declining fitted trend crosses the significance threshold. Re-running the identical method on raw death counts (`fit_baseline_trend`/`compute_deviations` on `deaths` instead of `age_adjusted_rate`) gives p=0.68, "No significant disruption" — passing cleanly, consistent with the a priori expectation. This is the same quantization mechanism suspected (but *not* confirmed — see the prior addendum) as a possible explanation for drowning's result; the two causes' outcomes on raw counts diverged (drowning stayed significant on counts, congenital malformations did not), which is itself the evidence that distinguishes a real effect from a rounding artifact. §7 method 4 is corrected: the negative control's pass/fail gate decision is computed on raw death counts, not age-adjusted rate; its charted/displayed series still uses age-adjusted rate for visual consistency with the other 7 series. This does not apply to the 6 substantive test causes (§3, §7a) — all have age-adjusted rates well above 20/100k, where 1-decimal rounding is a much smaller fraction of the signal and the original rate-based method (chosen because it controls for the population's age structure changing over 1999–2024, unlike raw counts) remains appropriate.

**2026-09-01 — Negative control has no *direct* mechanism, not "no plausible mechanism at all."** A self-critical review raised a fair objection: congenital malformations mortality isn't fully insulated from pandemic disruption, since prenatal and obstetric care (routine prenatal visits, anomaly screenings) was also disrupted during 2020–2021, and that could in principle affect either detection or outcomes of congenital anomalies. This is a real, if plausibly small and indirect, pathway that §7 method 4's original framing ("no plausible mechanism") understated. Retained as the negative control anyway — it remains far more insulated from adult pandemic-era behavior (isolation, deferred elective care, economic stress, direct viral injury) than any of the 6 substantive test causes, and it still passed cleanly on its gating metric (§ addendum above) — but "no direct mechanism" is the more honest framing than "no plausible mechanism."

**2026-09-01 — Independent cross-check now runs all three documented methods; the pipeline previously only ran two.** Found during a self-audit: §7 method 3 has always documented PELT, binary segmentation, *and* segmented regression as the cross-check, but `scripts/run_covid_disruption_pipeline.py` only ever imported and called PELT and binary segmentation — `fit_segmented_regression` (implemented and tested in `src/analysis/changepoints.py` since the diabetes-pilot phase) was never wired in. Fixed: all three now run, and `cross_check_methods_agreeing` (0–3) is tracked alongside the existing boolean. This changed a real, previously-reported number: Diabetes mellitus, previously reported as one of "3 of 6 test causes" whose cross-check didn't confirm ~2020 (PELT/binseg alone), is confirmed by segmented regression (1 of 3 methods) once it's included — so the real count is 2 of 6 (Diseases of heart, Cerebrovascular disease), not 3. All app text, `findings.md`, and the PDF report generator have been corrected to match.

**2026-09-01 — Heterogeneity-stage FDR correction was pooling both causes into one family; now corrected per cause.** §10 states FDR correction applies to context-variable comparisons "for a given cause," implying diabetes's 5 tests and drug overdose's 5 tests should each be their own family. The pipeline was instead pooling all 10 into one family before applying `benjamini_hochberg`. Verified this didn't flip any `fdr_significant` flag on the real data (checked both ways before fixing), but it wasn't what was documented and isn't guaranteed to stay inconsequential. Fixed to correct each cause's 5 comparisons as its own separate family, per §10 as written.

**2026-09-01 — County-level rurality finding: real selection bias found, and it affects the two test causes differently.** A self-critical review of the heterogeneity stage's most counterintuitive result (higher rurality → *less* disruption) found that `compute_county_disruption`'s `min_years_each_period` filter is not a random sample of counties: it excludes low-death-count counties, which are disproportionately rural. Measured directly (`src/analysis/heterogeneity.compute_selection_bias`, `outputs/models/heterogeneity_selection_bias.parquet`): counties *excluded* from the diabetes analysis are 77.7% rural on average, vs. 31.5% for the counties *included*; for drug overdose, 74.4% vs. 23.8%. The regression describes suburban/small-city counties far more than it describes rural America.

A second check (`check_within_sample_robustness`, `outputs/models/heterogeneity_rurality_robustness.parquet`) splits the *included* counties at their own median rurality and re-fits the rurality/disruption relationship in each half separately, to see whether the pooled result is driven by the whole range or just part of it. The two test causes diverge here in a way the pooled slope alone completely hides:
- **Diabetes mellitus**: the relationship holds up, and is if anything *stronger*, among the more-rural half of the included sample (slope −9.15, p=0.0032, n=463) than the less-rural half (slope −4.97, p=0.18, n=463, not significant). This doesn't cover the excluded, most-rural counties, but it's not evidence the finding is a pure artifact of urban/suburban variation either.
- **Drug overdose**: the opposite pattern — the relationship is driven almost entirely by the *less*-rural half (slope −35.05, p=0.00016, n=320) and is not significant at all among the more-rural half of the included sample (slope −3.48, p=0.40, n=321). This finding should not be reported with the same confidence as diabetes's; it looks more like "very urban counties differ from moderately urban counties" than a genuine rural-vs-urban gradient.

Both diagnostics are now surfaced directly on the Geographic Heterogeneity page next to the rurality result, not just documented here.

**2026-09-01 — Diseases of heart and Cerebrovascular disease's baseline window corrected from 1999–2019 to 2010–2019.** The trend-shape sensitivity check above found these two causes' significance didn't survive a curved baseline, but only reported *that* the conclusion changed, not *why*. Investigating produced three independent pieces of evidence, none of which reference any 2020+ data:

1. An F-test comparing a linear vs. quadratic fit on 1999–2019 data alone found overwhelming curvature for these two causes (heart disease F=222.7, cerebrovascular disease F=162.6, both p<0.00001) and essentially none for the other four test causes (F<9.5, and under 0.1 for Alzheimer's and cancer).
2. The linear model's own fitted value at 2019 was already far below the real observed value — 17.7 points low for heart disease (143.8 vs. actual 161.5), 5.8 low for cerebrovascular disease (31.2 vs. 37.0) — meaning part of the "2020 gap" this project was attributing to the pandemic already existed in 2019, before the pandemic, purely from extrapolating a steep early-2000s decline that had already flattened out by the 2010s.
3. A quadratic fit tracks 1999–2019 history almost perfectly for both causes (off by only 1.9 and 0.7 at 2019) but was rejected as the fix: extrapolated forward to 2020–2024 it predicts *rising* rates for both (heart disease 164.5→175.2, cerebrovascular disease 38.5→43.7), the standard failure mode of polynomial extrapolation and arguably a worse problem than the one it was meant to solve.

A shorter, more recent linear window (2010–2019) resolves this without either defect: it matches these two causes' flattened recent trajectory (confirmed by the same F-test finding no meaningful curvature within just this shorter window) and extrapolates as a stable, sensible straight line rather than a reversing curve. Re-running the primary method on this corrected window for these two causes only (`BASELINE_START_YEAR_OVERRIDES` in `scripts/run_covid_disruption_pipeline.py`; all other causes are unaffected):

| Cause | Old (1999–2019) | New (2010–2019) |
|---|---|---|
| Diseases of heart | p=6.33×10⁻⁴, +26.1% / +34.9% | p=3.86×10⁻⁵, +7.8% / +3.0% |
| Cerebrovascular disease | p=1.42×10⁻³, +37.0% / +57.3% | p=1.69×10⁻³, +8.8% / +6.1% |

Both remain a significant "Persisted" disruption — heart disease more confidently so than before — but the magnitude drops from an overstated 26–37% to a defensible 8–9%, and the 2024 trajectory reverses from "getting worse" to "drifting slightly back toward trend," which is the more plausible reading given four more years have passed.

Re-running the sensitivity checks against this corrected baseline: heart disease is now fully robust (agrees with both the alternate window and the quadratic-shape check). Cerebrovascular disease is substantially improved (quadratic p moves from 0.37 to a much closer 0.096, still short of significant) but picks up a new sensitivity to the significance-threshold axis — at α=0.01 its three-way classification shifts to "Resolved," because its individual 2022–2024 deviations no longer each clear a tighter 99% interval, even though the underlying acute-period p-value (0.0017) still clears α=0.01 comfortably on its own. This project reports that honestly rather than treating the correction as having fully resolved cerebrovascular disease's uncertainty: heart disease's finding is now on solid footing; cerebrovascular disease's is real and much better-supported than before, but remains this project's single most uncertain "Persisted" classification.

Deliberately **not** done: switching to a curved (quadratic) or percentage-decline (log-linear) baseline as the primary method for these two causes, despite quadratic fitting history better and log-linear partially closing the same 2019 gap (12.0 and 4.2 points respectively, an improvement over linear's 17.7/5.8 but not as complete a fix as the window correction). Both were evaluated and set aside for the reasons above (extrapolation risk for quadratic; incomplete fix for log-linear) — this is a same-family correction (still an ordinary least-squares straight line, same 2 parameters, same statistical machinery, just a different window), not a switch to a fundamentally different or more flexible model chosen because it happened to change an answer.

**2026-09-02 — Added a secondary, non-gating full-period (2020–2024) p-value alongside the primary acute (2020–2021) p-value.** A reader question ("shouldn't we test all the post-2020 years, since COVID's effects on other causes plausibly keep evolving?") was investigated by computing `compute_acute_pvalue(trend, deviations, (2020, 2024))` for all 6 test causes — the same function §7 method 1 already uses for the acute window, called with a wider year range, no new statistical machinery required. Every cause's p-value gets smaller (more significant) when pooling all 5 years instead of just 2: Alzheimer's disease flips from clearly non-significant (p=0.81, +1.0% acute deviation) to highly significant (p=0.0019, averaging −8.7% across 2020–2024) — corroborating the §3/app finding that its decline only became individually significant in 2023–2024, well after the pre-registered acute window. This is now surfaced in the app (`app/app_pages/causes.py`) as a "p-value (2020–24)" metric shown next to the primary p-value, plus a "Delayed disruption" badge and explanatory callout for Alzheimer's specifically.

This is deliberately **not** used to replace ACUTE_YEARS as the classification gate (§7 method 2) or the FDR family (§7a): averaging 5 years together can just as easily hide a real reversal as reveal a delayed one. Drug overdose spikes +40.9% acutely, then swings to −4.3% by 2024 — pooled into one full-period number it still reads "significant, +31.1%, direction unchanged," which erases the fact that the direction flipped. That distinction — Persisted vs. Reversed — is exactly what `classify_persistence`'s three-way scheme (§ above) exists to preserve, and a single pooled metric would flatten it back into the two-way "still elevated" reading this project already rejected once. The full-period p-value is reported as an additional, clearly-labeled lens, never as a replacement for the pre-registered primary test.

The trajectory chart's shaded prediction-interval band also gained a filled "gap ribbon" between the observed and expected lines for the tested years, colored red where observed ran above trend and blue where below, after a reader correctly pointed out that some significant results (diseases of the heart, cerebrovascular disease, malignant neoplasms) show two visually close lines on the chart, while at least one non-significant result (Alzheimer's disease, acute-only) shows a visually larger gap — an apparent contradiction that is fully explained by each cause's own prediction-interval width (driven by how noisy or smooth its 1999–2019 baseline was), not by any error in the fitted line. The ribbon makes the actual deviation magnitude visible without requiring the reader to eyeball the space between two overlapping lines; it doesn't change how significance itself is computed.

**2026-09-02 — Added an autocorrelation-robust (Newey-West/HAC) version of the primary acute p-value, after concluding the existing disclosure of this limitation (§12) materially understated it.** The primary method's prediction-interval math (`compute_acute_pvalue`) assumes each baseline year's deviation from the fitted line is independent noise. §12 already disclosed that measured lag-1 autocorrelation is large for several causes (0.50–0.82), but only as a one-line caveat ("reported p-values are likely optimistic"). A rough illustrative check — treating the autocorrelation as reducing the effective sample size via the standard AR(1) formula `n_eff = n(1-ρ)/(1+ρ)`, then inflating the reported standard error accordingly — suggested this could be severe enough to flip 3 of the 5 significant causes (diabetes, drug overdose, cerebrovascular disease) to non-significant. That heuristic is not a valid derivation for this setting (the AR(1) effective-n formula is for the variance of a sample mean, not a regression prediction interval), so a proper correction was built instead: `compute_hac_pvalue` in `src/analysis/excess_mortality.py`.

The corrected test keeps the identical fitted trend line and the identical acute (2020-2021) window; only the standard error changes. Two pieces replace the classical formula's single `residual_std * sqrt(1 + 1/n + ...)` term: (1) a Newey-West sandwich covariance matrix for the regression coefficients (Bartlett-kernel-weighted, small-sample lag length per Newey & West 1994's `floor(4*(n/100)^(2/9))` rule, giving 1-2 lags for this project's 10-21-year baselines), giving autocorrelation-robust uncertainty in the fitted line's own height; and (2) the variance of the mean of the two new acute-year observations, computed directly and exactly from the baseline's own empirical lag-0 and lag-1 autocovariances (`Var(mean of 2 correlated draws) = (γ₀+γ₁)/2`) rather than assumed independent. This is exact for a 2-year window, which is all this project's `ACUTE_YEARS` ever is; `compute_hac_pvalue` raises rather than silently generalizing the formula to a different window size.

Results on the real data:

| Cause | Classical p | HAC p |
|---|---|---|
| Diseases of heart | 3.86×10⁻⁵ | 2.70×10⁻⁵ |
| Diabetes mellitus | 7.99×10⁻⁷ | 6.66×10⁻⁵ |
| Alzheimer's disease | 0.807 | 0.857 |
| Cerebrovascular disease | 1.69×10⁻³ | 2.05×10⁻³ |
| Drug overdose | 5.98×10⁻⁸ | 2.53×10⁻⁵ |
| Malignant neoplasms | 1.94×10⁻⁴ | 6.59×10⁻⁴ |

The properly-derived correction is much less alarming than the rough heuristic suggested: **all 5 causes previously significant remain significant.** Heart disease and cancer (low measured autocorrelation, 0.12–0.19) barely move. Diabetes, drug overdose, and cerebrovascular disease (autocorrelation 0.50–0.82) see their p-values rise by roughly 1-2 orders of magnitude — a real, honest widening of the uncertainty this project should have modeled from the start — but every one of them still clears α=0.05 by a comfortable margin. Alzheimer's disease remains clearly non-significant either way. This is reported as a third p-value alongside the primary and full-period ones (`app/app_pages/causes.py`), not a replacement for the primary test: HAC's own asymptotic justification is large-sample, and this project's 10-21-year baselines are small, so it is offered as the more honest uncertainty estimate, not a new gate.
